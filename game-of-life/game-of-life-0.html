Today I wanted to talk to you about the work of one of my favorite mathematicians, John Conway. A brilliant man who had a unique perspective on mathematics, and whose tragic death during the pandemic I deeply mourn. Of all his creations and discoveries, the most famous is surely the one I’m going to tell you about: The Game of Life. 1 ~ Game? Life?

1.1 ~ A game without a player?

Conway was an avid player. He loved games, but also appreciated their relevance to mathematics education. He enjoyed spending time playing Go and Backgammon with his colleagues during tea time (yes, he was English) in the common rooms at the University of Cambridge. In the 1970s, Conway became interested in cellular automata. Think of them as an abstractly formalized sandbox studied through the lens of mathematics: a grid of “cells” that change their state (alive or dead, black or white, 1 or 0, etc.) step by step based on simple rules and the states of neighboring cells. Once you set the initial state, a cellular automaton runs on “its own” since each cell updates from one generation to the next based only on its current state, its neighbors’ states and the fixed rule set. The player makes no decisions2, and can neither win nor lose. For this reason, it is said to be a zero-player game, i.e. a simulation game that does not require a player.

What particularly interested Conway was how minimal and predetermined rules could give rise to complex behaviors. He spent months testing rule variations until he found the simplest rules that allow the emergence of life-like behavior. It’s important to remember that in the 1970s, computer simulations were not common, so Conway and his colleagues tested their rules by manually simulating the game’s states on a Go board. A good choice for a two-state (alive or dead) 2D grid: each square on the board represented a cell, and the piece placed on it represented the cell’s state (for example, white for alive and black for dead), and each cell depended on the state of its eight neighboring cells.

In the late 1970s, Conway had discovered the “perfect” rules that allowed him to tune the birth and survival thresholds, so populations would neither explode chaotically into infinity nor die out too quickly.
There are only 4 rules to follow at each stage between transitions:

  1. (Underpopulation) If a living cell has fewer than two living neighbors, it dies.
  2. (Overpopulation) If a living cell has more than three living neighbors, it dies.
  3. (Survival) If a living cell has two or three living neighbors, it remains alive.
  4. (Reproduction) If a dead cell has three living neighbors, it becomes alive.

1.2 ~ Emergence?

By defining these 4 simple rules, Conway sought to show that the emergence of complex patterns was possible. Not only do such patterns exist, but there are also many of them. So many, in fact, that a genuine taxonomy has been developed to classify them according to their behaviour3. Without any central control, these minimal rules make it possible to generate self-organizing structures whose complexity causes them to behave like living systems: just as chemistry gives rise to biology without an external blueprint.

Conway spent months with his students brute-force manual testing initial configurations (known as seeds) and observing their behavior. What is remarkable about this game is that, even though the rules are deterministic and predetermined, it is impossible to predict the behavior of the cells without simulating it.

There are three main types of patterns: still lifes (the ones that do not change from one generation to the next), oscillators (the ones that cycle through a fixed sequence of states; for example, Figure 2 shows an oscillator of period 2, meaning it returns to its original shape and position after 2 steps) and spaceships (the ones that translate themselves across the grid; see e.g. Figure 3).

Some patterns are significantly more complex because they can grow or produce smaller structures. These are then classified into categories such as guns or breeders.
